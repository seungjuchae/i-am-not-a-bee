Honeybees live in colonies that can contain tens of thousands of individuals. Most of them are female workers, a few hundred are male drones, and there is usually a single queen. The workers build the comb, gather food, and care for the young, while the queen lays the eggs.

A worker honeybee changes jobs as she ages. Young workers clean cells and feed larvae. Later they build comb, process nectar, and guard the entrance. In the last weeks of their lives they become foragers and fly out to collect nectar, pollen, water, and resin.

The waggle dance is one of the most studied animal behaviors. A returning forager runs in a figure eight on the comb, shaking her abdomen during the straight part. The angle of that run relative to vertical shows the direction of the food relative to the sun, and its length signals the distance.

Honey is made from nectar. Foragers carry nectar in a special stomach and pass it to house bees, who add enzymes and spread it in cells. The bees then fan their wings to evaporate water until the sugar content is high enough. Finally they seal the cell with a cap of wax.

Beeswax is produced by glands on the underside of a worker bee's abdomen. The wax appears as small flakes, which the bees chew and shape into hexagonal cells. The hexagon uses the least wax to store the most honey, a fact noticed by mathematicians as early as ancient Greece.

Bees are important pollinators. As a bee moves from flower to flower, pollen sticks to the hairs on its body and is carried to the next blossom. Many crops, including apples, almonds, blueberries, and squash, depend heavily on bees for good harvests.

The queen bee is the only fertile female in a typical colony. She can lay more than a thousand eggs a day in summer. Workers recognize her by her pheromones, chemical signals that keep the colony organized and discourage workers from raising new queens.

In winter, honeybees do not hibernate. They form a tight cluster inside the hive and generate heat by shivering their flight muscles. The bees on the outside of the cluster slowly rotate with those on the inside. The colony survives on the honey it stored during the warm months.

Swarming is how honeybee colonies reproduce. When a hive becomes crowded, the old queen leaves with about half the workers. The swarm settles temporarily on a branch while scout bees search for a new cavity. The scouts then hold a kind of vote through their dances before the swarm moves.

A honeybee's sting is barbed. When a worker stings a mammal, the barbs catch in the skin and the sting tears away from her body, which kills her. For this reason bees usually sting only to defend the colony. Bumblebees and many other bees have smooth stings and can sting more than once.

Bees see colors differently from humans. They cannot see red well, but they can see ultraviolet light. Many flowers have ultraviolet patterns, called nectar guides, that are invisible to people but lead bees toward the center of the flower.

Beekeeping has a long history. Ancient Egyptians kept bees in clay pipes, and medieval Europeans used woven straw skeps. The modern movable-frame hive was introduced in the nineteenth century and allowed beekeepers to inspect and harvest the comb without destroying the colony.

Bumblebees are larger and hairier than honeybees and live in much smaller colonies, often in the ground. They can fly in cooler weather and perform buzz pollination, vibrating their bodies to shake pollen loose from flowers such as tomatoes and blueberries.

Colony collapse disorder is the name given to the sudden loss of most worker bees from a hive. Researchers have linked declines in bee health to several factors, including parasitic mites, viruses, pesticides, poor nutrition, and the loss of wildflower habitat.

Drones are the male bees of a colony. They do not collect food and have no sting. Their main role is to mate with queens from other colonies. In autumn, when food becomes scarce, workers often push the remaining drones out of the hive.

Propolis is a sticky material that bees make from tree resin. They use it to seal small gaps in the hive and to coat the inside walls. Propolis has antimicrobial properties, and it helps protect the colony from fungi and bacteria.

A forager bee may visit hundreds of flowers on a single trip and make many trips a day. Over her short summer life, a worker produces only about a twelfth of a teaspoon of honey. A strong colony, however, can store many kilograms of honey in a good season.

Bees regulate the temperature of the brood nest very precisely, keeping it near thirty-five degrees Celsius. On hot days workers fan their wings at the entrance and spread water droplets on the comb so that evaporation cools the hive.

There are more than twenty thousand known species of bees. Most of them are solitary, meaning each female builds her own small nest and raises her young alone. Mason bees, leafcutter bees, and mining bees are common solitary bees in gardens.

Gardeners can help bees by planting a variety of flowers that bloom from early spring to late autumn. Native wildflowers, herbs such as thyme and borage, and flowering trees provide nectar and pollen. Avoiding pesticides and leaving some bare soil for ground-nesting bees also helps.

Honeybees communicate largely through smell. Guards at the entrance check the scent of returning bees to keep out intruders. When a bee stings, it releases an alarm pheromone that smells a little like bananas and alerts other bees to danger.

Different flowers produce honey with different colors and flavors. Clover honey is light and mild, buckwheat honey is dark and strong, and orange blossom honey is fragrant. Some honeys, such as manuka from New Zealand, are prized for special properties.

The honeybee colony is sometimes described as a superorganism. No single bee can survive for long on its own, but together the colony behaves like one living body, controlling its temperature, defending itself, and reproducing by swarming.

A beekeeper usually wears a veil and gloves and uses a smoker when opening a hive. Smoke masks the alarm pheromone and encourages bees to eat honey, which makes them calmer. Experienced beekeepers move slowly and avoid crushing bees.

In early spring, honeybees rely on the first blooming plants, such as willow, crocus, and dandelion, for fresh pollen. The pollen provides protein for the growing larvae, and the colony expands rapidly as more food becomes available.

Honeybees usually forage within a few kilometers of their hive, but they can fly much farther when food is scarce. They navigate using the position of the sun, the pattern of polarized light in the sky, and familiar landmarks.

Royal jelly is a secretion produced by young worker bees. All larvae receive some royal jelly at first, but only larvae chosen to become queens are fed it throughout their development. This rich diet is what turns an ordinary female larva into a queen.

Wasps are often confused with bees. Wasps are usually less hairy, have narrow waists, and many of them hunt other insects. Bees, by contrast, are covered in branched hairs that trap pollen, and they feed their young on pollen and nectar.

A single honeybee colony may collect pollen from dozens of plant species over a season. Scientists can identify which plants bees visited by examining pollen grains under a microscope, a method used to study both bee nutrition and the origin of honey.

In many cultures bees have symbolic meaning. They appear in ancient Egyptian royal titles, in Greek myths, and in medieval coats of arms. The beehive has long been used as a symbol of hard work, cooperation, and community.
